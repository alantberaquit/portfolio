Discussion · Common warning signals

Attackers often exploit pressure before they exploit technology.

Signal 01

Unexpected urgency

“Act in 15 minutes,” “your account will be closed,” or “pay this immediately” can be designed to make you react before verifying.

Signal 02

Sender mismatch

The display name may look familiar while the actual address or domain is slightly different: company-support.net instead of the real company domain.

Signal 03

Credential requests

Messages that ask you to enter your password, recovery code, or MFA code into an unexpected page deserve immediate scrutiny.

Signal 04

Unexpected attachments or links

Files and links can carry malware or send you to a fake sign-in page, especially when you were not expecting them.

Signal 05

Impersonation

An attacker may pretend to be a manager, IT support, a vendor, a bank, or a delivery service to create authority or familiarity.

Signal 06

MFA fatigue

Repeated login approval prompts you did not initiate may indicate someone already has your password and is trying to get you to approve access.

UrgencyThe message creates a short deadline to discourage careful verification.
Credential trapThe link points to an unrelated domain that could imitate a login page.
Discussion · Safe response

When something feels wrong, verify outside the message.

Step 01

Do not use the suspicious link

Open the known service or application independently instead of following the message's link.

Step 02

Verify through a trusted channel

Contact the person or team using a known number, directory entry, or official channel—not the contact information provided in the suspicious message.

Step 03

Report it

Use the organization’s phishing-reporting or security process so the message can be investigated and others can be protected.

Step 04

If you already interacted, act quickly

Report what happened immediately. If credentials may have been exposed, follow the organization’s password and incident-response process.

What the attacker may be trying

  • Using a password that has already been compromised
  • Sending repeated prompts until the user approves one
  • Relying on annoyance, confusion, or habit
